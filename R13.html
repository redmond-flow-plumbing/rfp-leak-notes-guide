What to Note About a Plumbing Leak Before You Call Us

You spot water where it shouldn't be — under the sink, on the ceiling, pooling by the water heater. Your first instinct is to call a plumber immediately, and that's fine. But spending five calm minutes on what to note about a plumbing leak before we arrive can be the difference between a one-visit fix and a return trip. Here's exactly what helps us help you faster.

First: Stop the Water, Then Document

Before you pick up your phone's camera, pick up the situation. If water is actively flowing, find the nearest shutoff — the fixture's supply valve (the little oval handle under the sink or behind the toilet) for a local leak, or the main shutoff where water enters the house for anything bigger. Can't find it? Call us first and we'll talk you through it while the truck rolls. Once the flow is stopped, mop up standing water with towels so we can see the actual source instead of a puddle. Then start your notes: the date and time you discovered the leak, whether it's constant or intermittent, and whether anything changed recently — a cold snap, a DIY project, a new appliance installation. Leaks have biographies, and the backstory points straight at the cause.

Photograph Everything, Twice

Take wide shots showing where the water is in the room, then close-ups of the suspected source — the valve, the joint, the stain, the puddle's edge. Photograph the water meter too, if you can: a spinning meter with everything off confirms an active leak and tells us its rough size. Snap the area again after you've mopped up, so we can compare. These photos do three jobs: they help us bring the right parts, they document damage for your insurance if you file a claim, and they give us a "before" picture if the leak behaves differently by the time we arrive. Intermittent leaks are notorious for hiding when the plumber shows up — your photos are the witness that doesn't go quiet.

Note the Details We Can't See Later

Some of the most useful information evaporates — literally. Is the water hot or cold? Hot points to the water heater or hot supply lines; cold points elsewhere. Does it smell? Musty suggests it's been happening a while; a rotten-egg note near a drain is a different problem entirely. What color is it — clear, rusty, or discolored? Rusty water from a ceiling stain often means a galvanized line or a failing water heater. Is the leak worse when something runs — the shower, the dishwasher, the washing machine? That correlation tells us whether it's a supply leak (pressure-driven, constant) or a drain leak (only when water flows through). Write it down now; you'll forget half of it by the time we're standing in your kitchen.

What to Tell Us When You Call

When you reach Redmond Flow Plumbing, lead with four things: where the water is, whether it's still flowing or you've shut it off, how long it's been happening, and whether the water is hot or cold. Mention your home's age and pipe material if you know it — "1970s house, I think the pipes are original" is genuinely useful. Tell us about access too: is the leak behind a wall, under the house, in a finished ceiling? That determines which tools and how much time to plan for. And be honest about what you've already tried — if you tightened something or poured something down a drain, we need to know so we don't get surprised.

After the Repair: Keep Your Records

Once the leak is fixed, don't delete those notes and photos — file them. Keep the repair invoice, your photos, and a one-paragraph summary of what happened in a home maintenance folder (digital or paper). This file earns its keep in three ways: if you file an insurance claim, the documentation proves the timeline and the professional repair; if you sell the house, disclosure forms ask about past water damage and your records turn a scary question into a clean answer; and if a related problem appears later, the history helps the next tech diagnose faster. We keep our own records of every job too, so if you ever need a copy of an invoice from two years ago, one call gets it.
